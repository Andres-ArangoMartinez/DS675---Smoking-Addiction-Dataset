# 2. About previous work

The studies in Section 1 can be read alongside the [selected Smoking Addiction Dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/datascikhan/the-nicotine-craving-and-anxiety-dynamics-dataset/data) because they address constructs, outcomes, and data structures that also appear in its documented variables. The dataset is described as **synthetic longitudinal data**, with 30 daily records for each of 1,000 simulated participants. Its fields cover nicotine-pouch use and craving, psychological states, daily activities, and participant characteristics. The comparison below follows four analytical axes derived from the recurring differences among the reviewed studies: **(1) psychological and behavioral factors; (2) predictive tasks and temporal horizons; (3) model evaluation and methodological limitations; and (4) data sources and generalizability**. These axes provide a way to explain what the literature tells us *about variables and measurements represented in this dataset*, while distinguishing a conceptual correspondence from evidence that the same empirical relationships exist in simulated records. They are an analytical organization of the reviewed work, not categories supplied by the studies or a statement of a future research task.

## 2.1 Psychological and Behavioral Factors Associated with Nicotine Craving and Use

A central connection is the overlap between the factors measured in the literature and the dataset's column groups. The dataset includes `Daily_Anxiety_Score`, `Perceived_Stress_Score`, `Mood_Score`, `Irritability_Score`, and `Restlessness_Score`, alongside craving measures such as `Overall_Craving_Score` and morning, afternoon, and evening craving scores. Spillane and Soyster examine person-specific relationships among affect, cigarette craving, and smoking, including earlier observations as covariates; McKinty and colleagues examine state anxiety and craving around nicotine-cue exposure in a laboratory setting ([J. A. Spillane and P. Soyster, 2023, Abstract; Data Preparation and Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1080/10826084.2023.2257312); [A. A. McKinty et al., 2026, Abstract; Statistical Analyses](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2025.108501)). These studies explain why anxiety and affect are relevant constructs in nicotine-use research, although the settings and instruments are not identical to daily synthetic scores.

The dataset also represents everyday behavior through `Sleep_Duration_Hours`, `Sleep_Quality_Score`, `Caffeine_Intake_mg`, `Exercise_Minutes`, and `Social_Interaction_Hours`, among other daily fields. Freeman and Gottfredson analyze daily sleep quality and craving in people receiving treatment for drug or alcohol use, separating differences between people from changes within a person and accounting for prior outcomes. Their findings concern substance use more broadly, not specifically nicotine pouches ([L. K. Freeman and N. C. Gottfredson, 2018, Abstract; Data Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2017.11.001)). Reed and colleagues study craving in relation to nicotine-product use: momentary craving was not significantly associated with subsequent use, while maximum craving was associated with use in the same assessment interval ([B. W. Reed et al., 2023, Abstract; §2.3 Statistical Methods](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2023.107803)). This distinction is relevant to a dataset that contains both craving scores and behavioral fields such as `Pouches_Per_Day`, `Pouch_Strength_mg`, and `Craving_Intensity_Peak`: overlap in variable labels does not establish the direction, significance, or timing of a relationship in these synthetic observations. Chapman and colleagues provide product-specific context for nicotine pouches, but their crossover study measures short-term nicotine exposure and subjective responses rather than daily longitudinal associations ([F. Chapman et al., 2022, p. 2931](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-022-06178-6)).

## 2.2 Predictive Tasks, Models, and Temporal Horizons

The dataset's repeated `Participant_ID` and `Day_Number` fields describe a **daily participant-level sequence**, not the minute-level or hourly sequence found in several published studies. It also includes separate morning, afternoon, and evening craving scores, but these named daily fields should not be equated with timestamped, repeatedly prompted ecological momentary assessments (EMA) or continuous sensor recordings. Fisher and Soyster use observations collected at several points per day to classify cigarette smoking approximately four hours later with person-specific Elastic Net and Naive Bayes models; Chatterjee and colleagues' *mCrave* estimates high-craving states between reports at minute-level resolution using mobile sensors and conditional random fields ([A. J. Fisher and P. Soyster, 2019, pp. 9–12](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/e24v6); [S. Chatterjee et al., 2016, Abstract; Findings](https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971672)). These studies examine related behavior at finer temporal resolutions than the dataset's daily records.

The **type of outcome** also matters. Perski and colleagues (2023) classify self-initiated lapse reports using information recorded at the same assessment, whereas their 2024 EMASENS analysis predicts lapse at a subsequent hourly assessment; Leppin and colleagues reuse the EMASENS cohort to examine binary lapse and high-craving outcomes under different measurement conditions ([O. Perski et al., 2023, pp. 1331–1332](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntad051); [O. Perski et al., 2024, pp. 7–8](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000594); [C. Leppin et al., 2026, pp. 5–7](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0349028)). Hébert and colleagues instead model time to a first lapse using survival analysis ([E. T. Hébert et al., 2021, Abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108340)). The course dataset includes craving and resistance-related fields, but its documented column inventory does not establish an equivalent clinical first-lapse event or a time-to-event endpoint. Lee and colleagues address next-day cigarette consumption, closer to the dataset's daily measurement scale but different from its nicotine-pouch consumption and craving scores ([J.-H. Lee et al., 2026, pp. 1883–1885](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41386-026-02448-5)). Nugroho's Kaggle notebook is the closest direct computational reference: it works with the synthetic dataset's daily craving and anxiety scores, including contemporaneous, lagged, and rolling inputs ([R. S. Nugroho, 2026, cells 18, 21, 29–37](https://www.kaggle.com/code/rizkisuryanugroho/time-series-forecasting?scriptVersionId=351856888)). These comparisons clarify which reported findings concern the same *kinds of measurements* and which concern different outcomes or time scales.

## 2.3 Predictive Performance and Methodological Limitations

Because the selected dataset contains repeated records for the same simulated individuals, its observations are structured by both **participant** and **day**. This is a meaningful point of comparison with how prior studies evaluate models. Fisher and Soyster use later observations from each individual's series for testing personalized smoking classifiers. Lee and colleagues primarily assess generalization across participants and also report a complementary temporal analysis. Those evaluation perspectives answer different questions, even when similar psychological variables are recorded ([A. J. Fisher and P. Soyster, 2019, pp. 11–14](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/e24v6); [J.-H. Lee et al., 2026, pp. 1883–1886](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41386-026-02448-5)). Lee's temporal comparison also found that adding mood, stress, and anxiety did not improve R² beyond a model containing consumption and craving measures. This provides an example of why a variable's substantive relevance does not guarantee incremental predictive performance in a particular evaluation.

Nugroho reports model results using this same type of participant-day structure, but the meaning of its errors depends on the code: the fitted models retain current-day explanatory measurements, while the separate persistence baseline uses a previous score. When its lag is missing, that baseline substitutes a mean calculated from held-out target values; another analysis applies `TimeSeriesSplit` after sorting first by participant and then by day, which is not a single global chronological ordering ([R. S. Nugroho, 2026, cells 18, 20–21, 26–27, 52](https://www.kaggle.com/code/rizkisuryanugroho/time-series-forecasting?scriptVersionId=351856888)). The reported performance therefore cannot be interpreted without distinguishing current-record estimation from prediction with earlier records. Kapoor and Narayanan describe how leakage or mismatched evaluation schemes can affect published machine-learning results; Davis and colleagues likewise show that whether an input constitutes label leakage depends on the timing and intended use of a model ([S. Kapoor and A. Narayanan, 2023, pp. 4–6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804); [S. E. Davis et al., 2024, pp. 274–276](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocad178)). Leppin's examination of feature selection, EMA frequency, and personal training data further illustrates that results depend on measurement and evaluation conditions rather than on algorithm choice alone ([C. Leppin et al., 2026, pp. 5–7](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0349028)). These methodological findings clarify what published metrics can—and cannot—tell us about repeated daily observations such as those in the course dataset.

## 2.4 Data Sources and Generalizability of Findings

The most consequential difference is that **the course dataset represents simulated nicotine-pouch behavior**, whereas most behavioral findings in the reviewed articles come from actual participants. Fisher and Soyster, Spillane and Soyster, and Perski and colleagues collected repeated real-world smoking reports, but used different schedules, instruments, populations, and outcome definitions ([A. J. Fisher and P. Soyster, 2019, pp. 9–12](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/e24v6); [J. A. Spillane and P. Soyster, 2023, EMA items](https://doi.org/10.1080/10826084.2023.2257312); [O. Perski et al., 2023, pp. 1330–1332](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntad051)). The 2024 EMASENS study additionally combines prompted assessments and wearable sensors; Leppin's 2026 analysis reuses that cohort rather than providing an independent sample ([O. Perski et al., 2024, pp. 5–8](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000594); [C. Leppin et al., 2026, pp. 5–6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0349028)). A similar column name, such as anxiety, sleep quality, or craving, does not establish that the dataset reproduces the same instrument, distribution, or relationship observed in those studies.

Other sources support more limited forms of comparison. McKinty's laboratory cue-exposure measurements are not interchangeable with everyday daily scores; Chapman's nicotine-pouch trial concerns short-term product effects, not longitudinal behavioral prediction ([A. A. McKinty et al., 2026, Statistical Analyses](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2025.108501); [F. Chapman et al., 2022, p. 2931](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-022-06178-6)). Ra and colleagues describe a longitudinal EMA and actigraphy protocol on sleep and smoking cessation, but report no findings from the planned study ([C. K. Ra et al., 2025, pp. 1–5](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0334129)). Adams and colleagues examine fidelity, privacy, and utility in synthetic patient data, a useful methodological context that does not independently verify the realism of the specific nicotine dataset ([T. Adams et al., 2025, pp. 1 and 4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.112382)). Thus, the reviewed literature helps interpret **why the dataset's recorded concepts resemble those studied in nicotine and substance-use research**, while drawing a clear boundary between findings from human participants and patterns represented in synthetic data. Neither clinical associations nor published performance figures can be assumed to transfer unchanged across that boundary.
