Act as a research librarian, systematic literature reviewer, data scientist,
and machine learning researcher. Conduct a broad, rigorous, and reproducible
investigation of prior work relevant to the following dataset and project:

Dataset:
“The Nicotine Craving and Anxiety Dynamics Dataset”
Kaggle URL:
https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/datascikhan/the-nicotine-craving-and-anxiety-dynamics-dataset/data

Project context:
We are working on a graduate-level supervised machine learning mini-project.
The dataset describes nicotine use, craving, anxiety, stress, sleep, mood, and
other behavioral or contextual variables across repeated participant-day
observations. We want to understand the existing work before defining a
well-supported predictive task and an original contribution.

The dataset appears to include variables such as participant ID, day number,
demographics, nicotine pouch use and strength, craving, anxiety, stress,
irritability, restlessness, sleep, caffeine consumption, exercise, screen time,
work, stressful events, and social interaction. Verify the dataset’s actual
contents and provenance rather than assuming this description is complete or
accurate.

Your goal is to identify and critically assess:

1. Repositories, notebooks, articles, or other projects that use the exact
   Kaggle dataset.

2. Related datasets, particularly those containing overlapping variables about
   nicotine or tobacco use, craving, anxiety, stress, sleep, mood, withdrawal,
   behavior, or environmental and social context.

3. Scientific papers and other credible research that use the exact dataset,
   use related datasets, or address closely related prediction problems.

4. Existing predictive tasks, modeling methods, validation designs, and research
   contributions that may overlap with our proposed project.

Do not limit your search to Kaggle. Search, where accessible and appropriate,
GitHub, GitLab, Zenodo, OSF, Hugging Face, Figshare, institutional repositories,
PubMed, Europe PMC, Crossref, OpenAlex, Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar,
publisher websites, and other relevant sources.

Research questions
------------------

A. Exact-dataset prior work

- Which public repositories, notebooks, papers, reports, or other projects
  explicitly use this dataset?
- Can the connection to the exact dataset be verified through a dataset URL,

- What predictive tasks do they address?
- What are their target variables, predictors, models, preprocessing steps,
  train/test splits, validation strategies, and reported metrics?
- Do they account for repeated observations from the same participant and the
  temporal order of observations?
- Are there potential data-leakage problems or other important limitations?
- Is the work executed and documented, or merely proposed?

C. Scientific literature and related work

Search for peer-reviewed papers, preprints, dissertations, and other credible
work on topics such as:

- Predicting nicotine craving, smoking, vaping, nicotine pouch use, lapse,
  relapse, or withdrawal.
- Relationships among craving, anxiety, stress, sleep, mood, irritability,
  restlessness, and nicotine consumption.
- Longitudinal, time-series, EMA, personalized, and just-in-time prediction.
- Group-level versus participant-specific or hybrid models.
- Feature selection, feature-group comparisons, ablation studies, and
  interpretable prediction.

For each important paper, identify:

- Full citation, DOI or stable link, publication status, and year.
- Research question and dataset or cohort.
- Variables, outcome, sample size, and study design.
- Predictive methods and evaluation approach, if applicable.
- Main findings and limitations.
- How directly it relates to our project.
- Whether data and code are available, with links.

Distinguish studies that examine associations from studies that actually train
and evaluate predictive models. Identify papers that reuse the same cohort so

Consider, where appropriate, temporal leakage, participant overlap between
training and test sets, same-day information unavailable at prediction time,

causal inference.

Do not assume that a method is novel merely because you did not find it.
Describe candidate contributions as preliminary until the relevant prior work
has been checked.

Evidence and search standards
-----------------------------

1. Start by verifying the Kaggle dataset’s identity, provenance, structure,
   variable definitions, and whether it is synthetic.

2. Use multiple search strategies: the exact title and URL, author names,
   distinctive filenames and column names, synonyms, related predictive tasks,
   citation chaining, and dataset or repository references.

3. Record the databases and platforms searched, example queries, date of search,
   inclusion criteria, and practical search limitations.

4. Prioritize primary sources: original papers, dataset records, codebooks,
   repositories, and study documentation. Use secondary sources to discover or
   contextualize evidence, and label them accordingly.

5. Provide direct links for every substantive finding. When feasible, inspect
   actual code, metadata, data dictionaries, or files rather than relying only
   on search snippets or repository descriptions.

6. Clearly label each finding as:
   - Verified directly;
   - Reported by a source but not independently verified;
   - Inferred; or
   - Unverified / not found.

7. Do not treat matching topic names as proof that two datasets are the same.
   Do not imply that a related real-world study was the source used to generate
   the Kaggle data unless the evidence establishes that connection.

8. Do not equate similar constructs without discussing differences. For
   example, momentary nervousness is not automatically equivalent to a daily
   anxiety score, and cigarette counts are not interchangeable with nicotine
   pouch use.

9. Be explicit when a file, article, repository, or full text could not be
   accessed. Do not invent variables, results, citations, or links.

10. Report negative findings with appropriate limits. “No source was found in
    the searches conducted” is different from proving that no source exists.

11. If a quantitative comparison is useful, report the denominator, unit of
    analysis, outcome, time horizon, and evaluation procedure. Do not compare
    metrics across incompatible tasks as though they measured the same thing.
